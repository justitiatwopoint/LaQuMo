The Double-Slit Experiment: Two Slits, One Great Mystery

Shine light through two narrow slits and you get stripes. That simple observation overturned Newton, launched the wave theory of light, and, once physicists fired single electrons through the slits, became the clearest window we have into the strangeness of quantum mechanics.

A doctor, a sunbeam and a card

At the start of the nineteenth century, most scientists followed Isaac Newton in thinking light was a stream of tiny particles, or corpuscles. Thomas Young disagreed. Young was a London physician with an astonishing range: he read a dozen languages, studied the mechanics of the eye, and later made some of the first real progress on Egyptian hieroglyphs from the Rosetta Stone. His biographer would call him "the last man who knew everything".

In lectures to the Royal Society between 1801 and 1803, Young showed that light from a single source, split into two parts and recombined, produces bright and dark bands. In one version he split a narrow sunbeam with the edge of a thin card. The drawing of two slits that appears in almost every textbook comes from his 1807 lecture course. The bands, he argued, were exactly what waves would do: where crest meets crest the light is brighter, and where crest meets trough it cancels.

The reaction was not kind. Newton's authority was enormous, and an anonymous reviewer in the Edinburgh Review, later known to be the lawyer and politician Henry Brougham, dismissed Young's papers in scathing terms. It took another decade, and a French engineer, for the wave theory to win.

The spot that should not exist

In 1818 Augustin-Jean Fresnel entered a prize competition of the French Academy of Sciences with a complete mathematical wave theory of diffraction. One of the judges, Siméon Denis Poisson, a convinced supporter of particles, worked through Fresnel's equations and found what he thought was a fatal flaw: they predicted that the shadow of a small round disc should have a bright spot at its very centre. Surely absurd.

François Arago, who chaired the committee, did the experiment. The spot was there. Fresnel won the prize in 1819, and the bright dot is still called the Arago spot, or, with some irony, Poisson's spot. (It had in fact been seen a century earlier, by Giacomo Maraldi in 1723, but nobody had understood what it meant.)

How the stripes form

Each slit acts as a new source of waves. At a point on the screen, the wave from one slit has travelled slightly further than the wave from the other. If the extra distance, the path difference, is a whole number of wavelengths, the crests arrive together and the light is bright:

\[ d\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots \]

Half a wavelength out of step, they cancel. For a distant screen, this gives evenly spaced bright fringes separated by

\[ \Delta y \approx \frac{\lambda L}{d}. \]

The numbers are small but not invisible. With yellow light (\(\lambda = 580\) nm), slits \(40\ \mu\text{m}\) apart and a screen 1 m away, the fringes are about 14.5 mm apart: easy to see by eye. Longer wavelengths spread the fringes out; wider slit spacings squeeze them together. Because each slit also has a finite width \(a\), the whole pattern sits inside a broader single-slit envelope, which is why the fringes fade towards the edges:

\[ I(\theta) = I_0 \cos^2\!\left(\frac{\pi d \sin\theta}{\lambda}\right)\,\mathrm{sinc}^2\!\left(\frac{\pi a \sin\theta}{\lambda}\right). \]

One photon at a time

In 1909 the young Geoffrey Ingram Taylor asked a pointed question: what happens if the light is made so feeble that, on average, there is hardly ever more than one "packet" of light in the apparatus at once? He dimmed the light of a gas flame with dark glass screens and photographed the fine interference fringes in the shadow of a sewing needle, the same wave effect that makes the double-slit stripes. The longest exposure took about three months. The fringes were exactly as sharp as with bright light.

Taylor's light was not yet a true one-photon-at-a-time source: that was only demonstrated cleanly by Philippe Grangier, Gérard Roger and Alain Aspect in 1986. But the lesson has held up in every experiment since. Each photon arrives at a single point, like a particle. Yet after thousands of arrivals, the points pile up into the stripes of a wave. Nothing in the individual hits tells you a pattern is coming; only the statistics do.

This is exactly what the Double-Slit Interference simulation in our Virtual Lab shows. Each coloured dot on the detector is one photon, landing at a random place chosen with the probability given by the formula above. At first the dots look scattered; within seconds, the fringes emerge. The curve beside the screen is the theoretical intensity the dots are building towards.

Matter waves

In 1924 Louis de Broglie made a daring proposal: if light waves can act like particles, perhaps particles can act like waves, with a wavelength set by their momentum, \(\lambda = h/p\). Three years later Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer saw electrons diffract from a nickel crystal, and de Broglie received the 1929 Nobel Prize.

The double slit soon followed, with ever heavier "particles":

  • 1961, electrons: Claus Jönsson in Tübingen cut microscopic slits in foil and recorded electron interference fringes.
  • 1974 and 1989, electrons one by one: Pier Giorgio Merli, Gian Franco Missiroli and Giulio Pozzi in Bologna, and later Akira Tonomura's team at Hitachi, filmed single electrons arriving one at a time and slowly building the pattern.
  • 1988, neutrons: Anton Zeilinger and colleagues sent neutrons through a double slit at the Institut Laue–Langevin.
  • 1991, atoms: Olivier Carnal and Jürgen Mlynek did it with helium atoms.
  • 1999, molecules: Markus Arndt, Anton Zeilinger and colleagues showed interference of C60 "buckyballs", made of 60 carbon atoms.
  • 2019, giants: the Vienna group interfered molecules of up to about 2,000 atoms, with masses above 25,000 atomic mass units.

Which way did it go?

The obvious question is which slit each electron "really" went through. You can find out: put a detector at the slits. But the moment you can tell, even in principle, which slit was used, the fringes disappear and you get two plain bands, as if the electrons were ordinary pellets. Knowing the path and seeing interference are mutually exclusive. Niels Bohr called this complementarity.

Richard Feynman opened the quantum volume of his famous lectures with this experiment, calling it a phenomenon that is impossible to explain classically and that holds "the heart of quantum mechanics". Later, "quantum eraser" experiments (proposed by Marlan Scully and Kai Drühl in 1982) showed that if the which-path information is recorded and then irreversibly erased, the interference can be recovered from the right subset of the data. It is subtle, but it never allows a message to be sent backwards in time.

Where it matters

Measuring the very small. Interference turns tiny differences in distance into visible changes in brightness. Interferometers measure wavelengths, the flatness of lenses and mirrors, and the thickness of thin films with nanometre precision.

Hearing the universe. LIGO is, at heart, a giant interferometer with 4 km arms. In 2015 it detected gravitational waves that changed the arm length by far less than the width of a proton, and its founders received the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Splitting light into colours. A diffraction grating is a double slit with thousands of slits. Gratings inside spectrometers identify chemical elements in stars, check the purity of medicines, and route signals of different colours through the optical fibres of the internet.

Sensing with atoms. Atom interferometers, the matter-wave cousins of the double slit, measure gravity and rotation so precisely that they are being developed for navigation without GPS and for mapping what lies underground.

Seven fun facts

  1. Voted the most beautiful experiment

    In 2002 readers of Physics World chose the double-slit experiment with single electrons as the most beautiful experiment in physics.

  2. The physicist who also read hieroglyphs

    Thomas Young worked out the meaning of several hieroglyphic signs on the Rosetta Stone before Jean-François Champollion completed the decipherment.

  3. The attempted disproof that proved it

    Poisson's bright spot was meant to show the wave theory was absurd. Instead it became one of the theory's most striking confirmations.

  4. A three-month photograph

    G. I. Taylor's dimmest exposure in 1909 lasted about three months, just to photograph the fringes around the shadow of a sewing needle in almost no light.

  5. Molecules made of 2,000 atoms interfere

    No experiment has yet found a size limit for quantum interference. Where, if anywhere, the quantum world gives way to the everyday one is still an open question.

  6. A double slit in time

    In 2023 a team at Imperial College London used laser pulses to make a thin film of indium tin oxide, the material in phone touchscreens, briefly reflective twice in quick succession: two "slits" in time instead of space. The interference pattern appeared in the light's colours instead of on a screen.

  7. You can try it at home

    Scratch two lines very close together through foil or a blackened piece of glass with a pair of taped-together razor blades, and shine a laser pointer through them onto a wall. Never look into the laser.
