The Bloch Sphere: One Picture for Every Qubit

A single sphere describes every possible state of a qubit, the spin of a proton in an MRI scanner, the polarisation of light in an optical fibre and the ticking of an atomic clock. Here is how it works, where it is used, and seven facts about it that still surprise physicists.

Where it comes from

In 1946 Felix Bloch published a paper on nuclear induction: atomic nuclei behave like tiny magnets, and in a magnetic field their magnetisation swings around the field direction like a spinning top. Bloch described this motion with a vector in three dimensions, governed by what are now called the Bloch equations. Working independently, Edward Purcell's group detected the same nuclear magnetic resonance that year, and the two shared the 1952 Nobel Prize in Physics.

The geometry is older than quantum mechanics. In 1892 Henri Poincaré represented every polarisation of light as a point on a sphere, now called the Poincaré sphere. In 1957 Feynman, Vernon and Hellwarth showed that any two-level quantum system can be pictured as a spin precessing in an effective magnetic field. Quantum computing inherited this picture, and today the sphere is the first thing most people learn about a qubit.

The fundamentals

A qubit has two basis states, \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\). Its general state is a superposition, and after removing an overall phase, which has no physical effect, every pure state can be written with just two angles:

\[ |\psi\rangle = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le \pi,\quad 0 \le \varphi < 2\pi . \]

Read \(\theta\) as latitude, measured down from the north pole, and \(\varphi\) as longitude: each state is a point on the surface of a unit sphere. The north pole is \(|0\rangle\), the south pole is \(|1\rangle\), and the equator holds the equal superpositions, such as \(|+\rangle = (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) on the \(x\)-axis and \(|{+i}\rangle = (|0\rangle + i|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) on the \(y\)-axis.

The picture also does real work:

  • Measurement is a projection. The probability of measuring \(0\) is \(\cos^2(\theta/2) = (1 + z)/2\), where \(z\) is the height of the point. States near the north pole almost always give \(0\).
  • Gates are rotations. Every single-qubit gate rotates the sphere. The NOT gate \(X\) is a half-turn about the \(x\)-axis, the Hadamard gate is a half-turn about the axis halfway between \(x\) and \(z\), and the phase gates \(S\) and \(T\) turn the sphere by \(90^\circ\) and \(45^\circ\) about \(z\).
  • The inside matters too. Noisy, partly-known states, called mixed states, lie inside the sphere, which is then called the Bloch ball. The centre is the state of complete ignorance: a perfect coin toss.
  • Time is motion. Under a magnetic field along \(z\), a state precesses around the vertical axis at a fixed latitude. This is the motion shown on our home page.

Why it matters in physics

Magnetic resonance. In nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and MRI, the Bloch vector is the measured magnetisation of countless nuclear spins. A radio-frequency pulse tips it away from the field, it precesses, and it relaxes back. Two relaxation times describe that return: \(T_1\), as the vector regrows along the field, and \(T_2\), as the spins lose step with one another and the sideways part shrinks. Different tissues have different \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), which is where MRI contrast comes from. The 2003 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Paul Lauterbur and Peter Mansfield for turning this into imaging.

Spin echoes. In 1950 Erwin Hahn found that dephased spins can be brought back into step: a half-turn pulse flips the fanned-out vectors across the sphere, and they reconverge into an "echo". The same trick, now called dynamical decoupling, protects qubits from noise today.

Rabi oscillations and atomic clocks. A steady drive rotates the state from pole to pole and back: a Rabi oscillation, named after Isidor Rabi (Nobel Prize 1944). Norman Ramsey's method of two separated pulses (Nobel Prize 1989) measures the precession frequency with extraordinary precision. It underlies caesium atomic clocks, and the SI second is defined as exactly 9,192,631,770 periods of the caesium-133 transition.

Why it matters in engineering

Building quantum computers. Engineers calibrate qubits by thinking in rotations: a "\(\pi\) pulse" is a half-turn that swaps \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\), and a "\(\pi/2\) pulse" moves a pole to the equator. Superconducting circuits, trapped ions and neutral atoms all implement gates as carefully shaped microwave or laser pulses that steer the Bloch vector.

Optical fibres and telecoms. Polarisation drifts as light travels through a fibre, which is a wandering point on the Poincaré sphere. Polarisation controllers and compensators are designed as sequences of rotations that bring it back to where it is needed.

Quantum cryptography. The BB84 protocol (Bennett and Brassard, 1984) sends single photons prepared at the poles or on the equator of the sphere. An eavesdropper who measures along the wrong axis unavoidably disturbs the states, and the legitimate users can detect it.

Medical imaging. Every MRI pulse sequence is, at heart, a choreographed set of rotations and waits for the Bloch vectors in the body, designed to bring out a particular \(T_1\) or \(T_2\) contrast.

Why it matters in materials science

NMR spectroscopy. The precession frequency of a nucleus shifts slightly with its chemical surroundings. Measuring these shifts, and how fast the Bloch vectors relax, reveals molecular structure and motion in liquids, polymers, batteries and catalysts.

Electron spin resonance. The same physics with electrons instead of nuclei (ESR, also called EPR) detects unpaired electrons: defects in semiconductors, radicals in chemical reactions, and the active sites of catalysts.

Quantum sensors in diamond. A nitrogen–vacancy (NV) centre is a defect in diamond whose electron spin can be prepared, rotated and read out with laser light, even at room temperature. How its Bloch vector precesses reveals tiny magnetic fields, so NV centres are used to map magnetism in thin films and currents in microchips at the nanoscale.

Muon spin rotation. In μSR, spin-polarised muons are implanted in a material and their spins precess in the local magnetic field. Their decay reveals that precession, giving a probe of magnetism and superconductivity from inside the sample.

Spintronics. The magnetisation of a nanomagnet in a hard drive or MRAM cell also precesses and relaxes (described by the Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert equation). There the vector is classical rather than a qubit, but engineers use the same picture of a vector moving on a sphere.

Seven facts that still surprise physicists

  1. Opposite points are "perpendicular" states

    \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) are orthogonal, at \(90^\circ\) to each other in the underlying space, yet they sit \(180^\circ\) apart on the sphere. That is why the formula uses \(\theta/2\): the sphere doubles every angle.

  2. A full turn is not enough

    Rotating a spin-\(\tfrac12\) particle by \(360^\circ\) returns it to the same point on the sphere, but multiplies its state by \(-1\). Only a \(720^\circ\) turn restores it completely. The sign is invisible on its own, but it shows up when the rotated particle interferes with an unrotated one. Neutron interferometry experiments confirmed it in 1975.

  3. Infinitely many states, but only one bit

    A qubit can be in any of a continuum of states, yet a measurement gives a single 0 or 1. Holevo's theorem (1973) proves that one qubit can never carry more than one bit of classical information.

  4. Light was there first

    The polarisation of light obeys exactly the same mathematics: horizontal and vertical polarisation at the poles, diagonal and circular polarisation around the equator. Polarising sunglasses are, in effect, a measurement on the Poincaré sphere.

  5. Paths leave a memory

    Steer a state around a closed loop on the sphere and it returns with an extra phase equal to half the solid angle the loop encloses (up to sign). This geometric phase depends only on the shape of the path, not on how fast it is travelled. It was found for light by Pancharatnam in 1956 and in general by Berry in 1984.

  6. Entanglement hides in the centre

    In an entangled Bell state, each qubit taken alone sits at the very centre of its sphere: individually, each looks like a perfect coin toss. All the information is in the correlations, which no single sphere can show. That is why our GHZ simulation draws its sphere for the pair \(|0000\rangle, |1111\rangle\) together.

  7. A four-dimensional sphere is hidden inside

    A qubit's normalised state is a point on a sphere in four real dimensions. Dropping the unobservable overall phase squeezes it onto the ordinary sphere. That map is the Hopf fibration, a celebrated object in topology in which every point of the Bloch sphere corresponds to a whole circle of states.
